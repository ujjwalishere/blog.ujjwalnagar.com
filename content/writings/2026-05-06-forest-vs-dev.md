# Response to a friend on Forests vs Development

![Screenshot](/images/forest_vs_dev.jpeg)  
Image: Screenshot of a post by a friend.

One dear friend of mine posted this today. It struck my immediately and I felt this urge to reply him immediately. This is not the first time I have seen such argument. Once I myself used to be a believer. Things have changed since then and now I feel I have a better understanding than before. If it were anybody else, I would have just skipped past it. Since he is a well-reasoned man, and a nice person, I feel he deserves some feedback. Also, almost 70% of the time I agree with his arguments, but I had my disagreements over time to time. I think we as a country ought to learn how to respectfully disagree.

Firstly, I would like to clarify that I do not consider myself as an environmentalist or anything. I always hated these tags and never made peace with any tags imposed upon me. I consider myself a reasonable person, who can think for myself. You can call me an Empathetic Rationalist if you insist. I also want to say that the term environmentalism means different things to different people, and we will get to that.
So, let’s get to the main arguments here:

1. We cannot expect a country to develop, because infrastructure need to be built; Hence we need to clear significant amount of forests.
2. Once a country is developed, they can restore forests.
3. If we do not develop, we will remain trapped as a developing nation.

These are the three main arguments he is making here, as I can see. I would say they are good arguments and do deserve merit. But I would also say how they also lead us into this zero-sum game type thinking.
Let’s get to them one by one –

*We cannot expect a country to develop, because infrastructure need to be built; Hence we need to clear significant number of forests.*

Plainly I agree with the fact that we need to build infrastructure if we want to develop as a country. Any country, society need to build infrastructure, after all we all want to live in a house, get treated in a hospital, and travel to find work. But it all relies on this idea of development. GDP is the most used indicator of development, but I do not need to tell you how [flawed](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/gdp-is-the-wrong-tool-for-measuring-what-matters/) it is, as a measure of development. Even the Kuznets(who came up with GDP) himself said that a nation’s welfare and quality of life cannot be inferred from GDP. Most of these measures of development are economic in nature and do not say anything at all about the actual development of a country.
Development in my eyes in simple terms should be about the welfare of people and better quality of life. No one should be living in poverty, and everyone deserves a decent lifestyle. These are synergistic with access to food, water, shelter, education but also to decent work and equality. So, we have to come up with better ways to measure development, and UN SDGs are one such attempt. There is a [whole debate](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/05/26/business/economy/gdp-united-nations-prosperity-measures.html?unlocked_article_code=1.2FA.x3DX.RbFZZLNjBUuQ&smid=url-share) on how we can better measure development.

The question of justice and equality matter the most, whose development are we talking about here. In case of India, the [top 10% of the Indian population holds 77% of the total national wealth](https://www.oxfam.org/en/india-extreme-inequality-numbers). Even if you talk about developed countries for e.g. US, such [extreme inequalities](https://www.forbes.com/sites/noahkirsch/2017/11/09/the-3-richest-americans-hold-more-wealth-than-bottom-50-of-country-study-finds/) are there. Some people like to plug-In Trickle-down economics here, but that has already been [settled](https://www.lse.ac.uk/research/research-for-the-world/economics/tax-cuts-for-the-wealthy-only-benefit-the-rich-debunking-trickle-down-economics).

It is also necessary to look at what infrastructure is important. Do we really need to clear our forest to build, schools, hospitals, trains. Whom does the infrastructure serves, does it make people’s lives better? Do we need a 10-lane highway in the middle of forest, where trains can do the job. Apparently, the main reason for clearing forest is not even the availability of land to build things. Agricultural expansion is the biggest reason, which mostly goes to feed animals then to later feed on them. These problems have their own solutions, one of which is how to better store food from getting wasted before it even reaches the plate. I would suggest to my friend to work on better food storage and transportation methods. Building infrastructure in India caused just [1.5% of forest loss](https://globalnaturewatch.org/blog/forest-insights/whats-happening-in-india-forests/), which itself can be avoided by better placement of infrastructure.

This assumption that we must [clear forest to develop](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7202948) is inherently wrong. This does not need to be the case; it depends on the choices we make on how we want to get developed. Even the act of clearing forest goes against the idea of development. Even if you are just talking in economic terms, forests provide a huge economic value. India’s forest alone provides around 2.5 trillion dollars’ worth of ecosystems services annually. This is obviously an underestimate because there is no way to, we can fully assess these services, because we do not [understand them](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2665972726001248).

I am not even talking about the moral, social and environmental costs that come from clearing the forest. The most vulnerable of society rely on these forests and ecosystems, and by clearing them we are making same people less developed, who most need our so-called development. The communities who have relied on forest for centuries, and we are just snatching it away from them in the name of development. Can we calculate the economic value of forest provided by storing the carbon, which would otherwise lead to certain degree of global warming with certain loss of GDP, we cannot.

*Once a country is developed, they can restore forests.*

His second argument has a flawed assumption, that we can grow forest back. This shows a lack of understanding about forests and what they are. We cannot grow forests back. Forests are not just bunch of trees, they are ecosystems. You can have a hectare of bunch of trees, or you can have a hectare of forest. Just by planting trees you do not get the forests back. These forests are one of life’s most complicated and intricate relationships which we do not even understand. Many species of plants, insects, fungi, animals which have built these intricate relationships over centuries and millennia, you cannot build that. Some people argue that as science will evolve, we will be able to do that. My argument is you cannot build what you do not understand. With the current extinction rate of species, we are headed for arguably the sixth mass extinction. [We still do not know 86% of the species on earth.](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/)

So many species will just disappear [before we even will get the chance to discover them.](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/how-many-species-can-we-find-they-disappear-forever-180950184/) Why does it matter here? Because of these species and their interactions, which make a forest forest. If we lose them, we lose forests. Why does it matter, because we depend on them, our food, our health, life itself depends on them. One of the classical examples of it is from Sweden. Some scientists from UK were found to be stealing short-haired bumblebee (sensationalized) from Sweden, which led to a huge outrage. I get it that it was planned but still why were they doing it. Just read about it why? Apparently, even if you have tons of money, you cannot bring species back. People who claim otherwise are lying, even if you can; think about economics of it. If you still are not convinced, read about [German forests](https://www.dw.com/en/german-forest-summit-3-ways-to-revive-dying-woods/a-58782039) most of which are planted forest.

When it comes to his third argument, I feel like I answered it in the first argument mostly. Yes we need to develop, but rethink what it actually means to develop. I would suggest my friend to read Amartya Sen’s Freedom as Development, even I have not read it completely but know the main argument.

In conclusion, This is not environmentalism; it is just common sense. Apart from all economics, forests also hold cultural and religious values. We never needed to become environmentalists because, culturally, we were told to preserve our environments. If you read about how environmentalism started, it did not start from India. It started when people felt the effects of destruction of forests. Even the economics of it became hard to ignore. It did not came out of our inherent altruism towards environment but from this realization that we and our economies depend on the services of forests and environment. Studies have shown that development and forests can go hand in hand, and many countries have already pledged that.
So, I would only say to my dear friend to read a little more and realize that this is not our generation who is at fault, but our previous ones. We are only the victims of their approach towards development.

His counter-arguments, neutrally summarized:

- **GDP**: Flawed but still the best growth metric we have; higher GDP countries generally have better quality of life.
- **Infra tradeoffs**: Highways and trains aren't either/or — both help GDP and quality of life. Shortest routes sometimes can't avoid forests without becoming impractical; shorter routes also cut emissions.
- **Institutions**: Environmental laws are strict enough; India is close to meeting its Paris Agreement targets.
- **Development vs QoL**: Challenges you to name a country with lower per-capita income than India but better quality of life.
- **Inequality**: Rising inequality isn't just the rich getting richer — it also creates stable jobs; developed nations had similar inequality during their growth phase.
- **Author bias**: Critics of Indian development are often from already-developed countries, whose comfortable present blinds them to the cost their own countries paid to get there.
- **Privilege**: Romanticizing forests/tribal life is easy from a screen; the people actually living it often want out. Same logic applies to factory workers.
- **No precedent**: No rich country got rich without cutting forests/polluting — sustainable development is a luxury of already-rich, small nations.
- **Final jab**: Both of you would still choose to live in the US/Europe over India, so the "development" debate is somewhat moot.
