## The Criticism of the Memo

Memo:
>"'Dirty' Industries: Just between you and me, shouldn't the World Bank be encouraging
>MORE migration of the dirty industries to the LDCs (Less Developed Countries)? I can
>think of three reasons:

This memo talks about moving the more polluting industries, which I am assuming are manufacturing industries like textile, mining, steel, chemical manufacturing, etc., to less developed countries. This is an internal Memo from the World Bank written by Larry Summers and Lant Pritchett. Their arguments here are purely in terms of financial rather concerned about issues involving justice, fairness, equality. The way it is being argued, it seems like a simple cost/benefit analysis devoid of any value judgments. On paper, it should be fine, but when you want to implement policies on the ground, then values do matter. 

All organisations are built on certain shared values on which we all agree. The World Bank itself has a code of ethics, which determines how the World Bank operates. Intentionally encouraging industries to pollute less developed countries goes against the ethical code of fairness and discrimination. People’s lives are precious and cannot be used as a variable in a cost-benefit analysis. 
These arguments actually set a good example of what the world is to come when we operate without any moral and ethical values. Yes! There was a lot of controversy and bad press about it, but it is also wrong to personally attack someone if their arguments are wrong. 

>Argument 1
>The measurements of the costs of health-impairing pollution depend on the foregone
>earnings from increased morbidity and mortality. From this point of view, a given amount
>of health-impairing pollution should be done in the country with the lowest cost, which
>will be the country with the lowest wages. I think the economic logic behind dumping a
>load of toxic waste in the lowest wage country is impeccable, and we should face up to
>that.

So, here the argument means that for any country, the cost of pollution can be determined by the loss of economic value (money) because the pollution is making people less productive by affecting their health. People dying is also considered a loss in terms of the earnings that person would have earned. They go on to say that such activity should be done in a country where the economic loss is minimal. Since people in less developed countries earn less, their lives have less value, hence the economic cost will be less, if I were to say in plain words. This is the logic they are trying to use to justify dumping toxic waste and polluting into poor countries. 

Here, the problem is very clearly visible in the argument. They are considering that people earn less than that means their lives have less value. This goes against all the values that most of us hold dear. Even the US Constitution, in which the World Bank operates, says “All men are born equal”. These values of equality are also enshrined in the constitutions of countries like India. Even if you are a billionaire or a farmer in rural India, the value of life is equal; it is not determined by how much you earn. This is where the argument falls on its face, doing such a thing goes against the Constitution of both the US and, for e.g. India in our case, even if you forget about our personal moral and ethical values. 

I am not sure about whether such a type of thinking has prevailed in the World Bank project, but these are definitely happening. For e.g. A recent report by “The Reporters' Collective” shows how India is becoming the world’s tyre furnace. A ton of used tyres are being imported to India, where they are being burned to extract raw materials. This process pollutes the local environment and severely affects the health of local people. All of this is happening because India does not have a regulatory framework to stop or regulate pollution, hence making it cheaper to pollute here. These tyres can be burned in developed countries themselves, but their regulations make it more expensive.

>Argument 2
>The costs of pollution are likely to be non-linear as the initial increments of pollution
>probably have very low cost. I've always though that under-populated countries in Africa
>are vastly UNDER-polluted, their air quality is probably vastly inefficiently low
>compared to Los Angeles or Mexico City. Only the lamentable facts that so much
>pollution is generated by non-tradable industries (transport, electrical generation) and that
>the unit transport costs of solid waste are so high prevent world welfare enhancing trade
>in air pollution and waste.

The core idea behind this argument is that a little bit of pollution is not very harmful. It becomes harmful when it crosses a certain threshold. Hence, the initial increment in pollution has a very low cost. Here, they mean that since many African countries are underpopulated, they have more room to absorb pollution without much real harm. In simple language, their clean air is a resource that is not being utilised efficiently. All this argument is based on the simple idea of supply and demand — if developing countries have clean air but developing countries don’t, then they can trade. Developed countries can pay developing countries to accept their pollution, and both sides will be better off financially. 

They also admit that such an approach also faces real-world barriers since things like power plants and cars can not be moved to another country. Shipping solid waste also becomes very expensive because of the cost of transportation involved. 
There are many problems with this argument, and a lot of assumptions are involved here. First, they are underestimating the cost of pollution of the receiving country; the cost of healthcare is just one. For example, how do you estimate the cost of pollution on a child’s development and education? How can we quantify the loss of potential a child has because he/she is not able to attend school because of constantly being sick from drinking polluted water? How do you estimate the cost of loss of pollinators, hence leading to lower yields in a nearby farmer’s field? These are not one-time costs; the upcoming generation will have to keep paying the cost. They get compounded over time.

The second problem is the assumption that all things can be compensated for by money. Can you compensate for the loss of a species, let’s say a fish or a bird that has gone extinct because of the pollution? How do you account for the loss of ecosystem services provided by that species? Does paying any amount of money bring that species back to life? Is that even possible to do with money?
There are a lot of assumptions and neglect that make this argument look plausible and economically sound, but it isn’t. First, try assessing all the costs mentioned, even if you can somehow add them all up and see if it still makes it economically viable. These are being born wither because of the neglect of the effect of pollution and true cost, or maybe they just don’t know about all these costs. It will not be appropriate to say they don’t care about it at all but there could also be a possibility of wilful neglect.

>Argument 3
>The demand for a clean environment for aesthetic and health reasons is likely to have
>very high income elasticity. The concern over an agent that causes a one in a million
>change in the odds of prostrate cancer is obviously going to be much higher in a country
>where people survive to get prostrate cancer than in a country where under 5 mortality is
>is 200 per thousand. Also, much of the concern over industrial atmosphere discharge is
>about visibility impairing particulates. These discharges may have very little direct health
>impact. Clearly trade in goods that embody aesthetic pollution concerns could be welfare
>enhancing. While production is mobile the consumption of pretty air is a non-tradable.
>"The problem with the arguments against all of these proposals for more pollution in
>LDCs (intrinsic rights to certain goods, moral reasons, social concerns, lack of adequate
>markets, etc.) could be turned around and used more or less effectively against every
>Bank proposal for liberalization."

Income elasticity measures how the demand for something changes when your income changes. Here, they are arguing that you are going to worry about a clean environment and only demand it when you have a good amount of income. Basically, they are arguing that a clean environment is a luxury good if I try to put it into simple words. A poor country will not worry about a tiny increase in risk of cancer risk when 200 out of 1000 children die before the age of 5. 

They also argue that the concern with air pollution is the problem of visibility. They said that this is not going to have much of an impact when it comes to health, which is clearly not true. They are trying to frame air pollution as a luxury concern being imposed as a universal standard. They go on saying that you can move the polluting factories to poorer countries, but you can not export clean air back, which they mean by being non-tradeable. Overall, they say this will be beneficial for both countries involved.
His final and most important point is that if you are arguing against their arguments based on moral, ethical, intrinsic rights, lack of adequate markets etc than those arguments can be used against almost all World Bank policies. Basically, they are saying that you can not be selective on applying these arguments here but not on other issues.

Here, they are right that it will be hypocrisy to criticise on some basis but apply the same points to other policies. But even if we leave the moral and ethical arguments aside, there are still a lot of other issues here. I can, to some extent, agree that you will not worry about a clean environment much (which is a big assumption) when you are struggling to put food on the table. When it comes to air pollution, it kills 1.7 million people annually and costs 9.5% of the GDP loss; it does not seem like a fair deal to India, leaving all qualitative arguments aside. It does not seem like a “little direct impact on health”. 

The major problem with all these arguments is giving up to the business as usual and not innovating. Poor people are being asked to suck up the pollution, while the technologies to mitigate such effects definitely exist. Even if they are lacking, instead of promoting innovation into new technologies to reduce such effects, we are being told to just accept such injustice.
They argue that this can be applied to all the projects, which may not necessarily be true, but you can still make everyone better off without infringing on the rights of anyone. Yes! It is challenging to stand up to moral and ethical calls, but the world of the future must stand on the foundation of morality, equality and justice. It is not something that we can trade off, given some minor increase in income.
